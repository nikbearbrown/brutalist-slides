brutalist-slides sample deck

What the evidence says about slides

A short talk that follows its own rules

This deck is the sample for brutalist-slides. Its content is the evidence base the skills are built on, and every slide is built the way that evidence recommends.

Audience: people who make slides for teaching or research and have read the usual advice.

The medium

Across 48 studies, teaching with slides made no average difference to learning.

Observed data

Source: Baker, Goodboy, Bowman and Wright, Computers and Education, 2018

On entering: the axis is an effect size. Zero means students learned the same with slides as without.

Step 1: the pooled estimate sits at 0.067, and its interval crosses zero. There was a benefit for school-age students and none for college students. The authors' advice: stop asking whether to use slides and study how they are used. That is what the rest of this talk does.

The makers

Of 140 decks scored against perception research, none was flawless.

Observed data

Source: Kosslyn, Kievit, Russell and Shephard, Frontiers in Psychology, 2012

On entering: these are shares of 140 slideshows from research, education, government and business, scored against 137 rules.

Step 1: the commonest faults are about working memory and legibility. Academic decks were no better than business decks.

Step 2: a caution on the numbers. One bad slide flags the whole deck, so these shares run high. The direction is right; the exact figures are soft.

Why rules are needed

Good slide design is not intuitive. It has to be made explicit.

In a second experiment, observers often failed to pick the flawed version of a slide. When they picked correctly they often could not say why. So we cannot rely on looking. We need stated rules, and measurements where we can take them.

Part one

What holds up

Four findings, strongest evidence first

Spatial contiguity

Placing words beside what they describe helped in 22 of 22 tests.

Observed data

Source: Mayer and Fiorella, Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning, 2014

On entering: each square is one controlled test of the same idea: put the label next to the thing, not in a legend or a caption somewhere else.

Step 1: all 22 came out the same way, with a large median effect. In practice: label lines and bars directly and delete the legend. One caution that applies to all of this research: the lessons were short and the tests immediate, so expect smaller effects in a real classroom.

Redundancy

The case against on-screen text shrank from 0.87 to 0.10 between two tallies.

Observed data

Sources: Mayer, Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 2017; Mayer, Multimedia Learning, 3rd edition, 2021

On entering: the claim was that adding printed text that repeats the narration hurts learning.

Step 1: in the 2017 review the median effect was large.

Step 2: four years later, 8 of 12 tests were positive and the median was near zero. What survives is narrower: text that repeats the speaker word for word hurts, and brief labels do not. Slide-design books rarely mention the retreat.

The working rule

Show what the room must see, and say what it must understand.

Conceptual illustration

The cleaner finding for a live talk is the modality principle: words that go with a picture work better spoken than printed. So the split is by job. The screen carries what has to be seen to be followed. The speaker carries the explanation. A sentence that is on the screen and also read aloud is doing neither job.

Legibility

Twenty-point type in PowerPoint is 40 pixels on a 1080-pixel canvas.

Computed

Source for the 20-point floor: Kosslyn, Clear and to the Point, 2007

Point sizes only mean something relative to the slide's height. Kosslyn's floor is 20 points on a standard slide. That is 3.7 percent of the height, so on this canvas it is 40 pixels. Two thirds of the decks in his sample had text smaller than that.

This is a derived rule, not a tested threshold. The better test is to shrink the slide on your monitor to the size the screen looks from the back row and try to read it.

Part two

What does not hold up

Rules that are repeated more than they were ever tested

Folklore

Three of the most repeated slide rules have no evidence behind them.

The ruleWhat tracing it found
Attention drops after ten minutesOne 1978 paper that barely measured attention
We remember 10% of what we readPercentages added later to a chart that had none
Six bullets of six words; 10/20/30Practitioner habits, never validated

Sources: Bradbury, Advances in Physiology Education, 2016; Subramony and others, Educational Technology, 2014

Stop here and give the room a moment with the table.

The attention-span figure: Bradbury followed the citations back and found a single paper, and note-taking in lectures stays fairly steady until near the end. The retention percentages were attached to Edgar Dale's Cone of Experience, which contained no numbers at all. The counting rules are habits someone found useful. None of them is a finding.

Takeaway

State a claim, show its evidence, and measure the rest.

The full evidence, with tiers and references: docs/evidence.md

Three things to take away. Make each headline a sentence that could be wrong. Put the evidence for it underneath, labelled where it is. And do not trust your eye for size and contrast: measure them.

The document carries what a deck should not: the effect sizes, the caveats and the references.
